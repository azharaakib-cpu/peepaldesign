Design for accessibility

We design products that work for everyone — regardless of how they see, hear, or navigate — built to WCAG standards from the start, not patched in after launch.

Usable by everyone, by design

What accessibility design means, and the four WCAG pillars every decision gets checked against.

Definition

“Built in, not bolted on.”

Accessibility design ensures a digital product can be used by everyone, including people with disabilities — through clear structure, sufficient contrast, keyboard navigation, and support for assistive technology like screen readers.

The four WCAG pillars
Perceivable
Content works regardless of how it's presented.
Operable
Every interaction works by keyboard, not just a mouse.
Understandable
Clear language and predictable navigation.
Robust
Reliable across browsers and assistive tech.

The four pillars, in practice

What perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust actually mean when you're shipping a screen.

Perceivable

Enough contrast, resizable text, and structure that survives however it's presented.

Operable

Every control reachable and usable by keyboard, with visible focus states.

Understandable

Plain language and navigation that behaves the way people expect.

Robust

Marked up so browsers and assistive tech read it reliably.

Cheaper, safer, and better for every user

Four reasons this belongs in the design phase, not a pre-launch fire drill.

Most issues start at design. The majority of accessibility problems can be prevented before a single line of code is written — a colour pairing, a focus order, a missing label are all decisions made on the canvas, not fixed later in the codebase.

Often a legal requirement. Many countries now mandate digital accessibility, with WCAG as the referenced technical standard, and enforcement has moved from advisory to actionable in most major markets.

Better for everyone. Clear contrast, simple navigation, and legible text improve usability across the board — captions help someone in a noisy room the same way they help someone who's deaf.

Avoids costly rework. Fixing accessibility after launch costs far more than designing for it from the start, since structural issues like navigation order are built into the product, not styled on top of it.

Beyond the four pillars

The four pillars, plus the contrast thresholds and user testing that keep them honest.

01

Perceivable

Content works regardless of how it's presented — text alternatives for images, and never depending on colour alone to convey meaning.

02

Operable

Every interaction works via keyboard alone, not just a mouse — visible focus states and no keyboard traps anywhere in the flow.

03

Understandable

Clear language and predictable navigation, so people always know where they are and what happens next before they click.

04

Robust

Works reliably across browsers, devices, and assistive technology — screen readers and voice input get markup they can actually parse.

05

Sufficient contrast

Text and interface elements meet real WCAG contrast thresholds, checked against the actual colours shipped, not eyeballed.

06

Tested with real users

Validated with people who use assistive technology daily, not just scanners that catch a fraction of what real usage reveals.
